# German quality on real content (2026-11-20)

How good the German learning pack (German → English, for English speakers learning German) is on real videos and articles at step L2-1 (the `de` pack and the `de-en` dictionary with its tagger and German grammar, no names rule, no highlighted phrases), and what the next steps should fix. Default settings: known level 3000, nothing saved. Method as in [french-quality.md](french-quality.md), whose steps L1-1 and L1-2 this step does at once; sources and gate measured in L0 ([translation-data-expansion](translation-data-expansion.md) "Round 2").

## Method
- **Corpus** (collected 2026-11-20; `data/raw/quality/2026-11-de/corpus/`, git-ignored, with `manifest.json`): 55,016 word tokens. Claude in Chrome was not connected for the whole session, so the corpus was collected without the user's Chrome: the YouTube tracks with `yt-dlp` (the user's own install; subtitle files only, no video) and the articles from their HTML or Wikipedia's API (`grab/yt-dlp-grab.py`, `grab/article-fetch.py`, the same JSON as the Chrome grabs). **No Netflix episode** could be collected without the browser then; C2 (2026-11-20, below) added five.
  - 19 YouTube videos (43,765 tokens): Germany, Austria; street interviews (Easy German), history and science explainers (MrWissen2go, MAITHINK X, Kurzgesagt, Doktor Whatson, Simplicissimus), news (tagesschau, tagesschau in Einfacher Sprache, ZIB 2), political satire (heute-show), documentary report (Y-Kollektiv), technology (Breaking Lab), food (Galileo, Sallys Welt), a let's play (Gronkh), a comedy vlog, a car review, a football press conference, stand-up (Felix Lobrecht, an hour). 6 manual tracks, 13 ASR. No English line: none has a human English track, and YouTube answered 429 to every translated track (as for the French study's ASR tracks).
  - 13 articles (11,251 tokens, each cut at about 1,500 words): four Wikipedia articles (Photosynthese, Marlene Dietrich, Brezel, Käsespätzle), tagesschau (2), Deutsche Welle, SRF (2), BR24, t-online (2), heise. Stern and GEO answered "Access Denied"; one heise article is behind its paywall: left out.
- **Pipeline**: the skill's `build-units.ts` (4,418 YouTube cards, 255 paragraphs) and `measure.ts` (`LEARNING=de NATIVE=en LEVEL=3000`) over this branch.
  - 5 Netflix episodes (15,894 tokens; C2, 2026-11-20, in the user's Chrome, profile KEISUKE, each muted and paused at once), one per show, the episode drawn with `random.Random(20261120)`: Dark S2E5, How to Sell Drugs Online (Fast) S4E1, Kleo S2E1, Barbaren S2E1, Liebes Kind S1E3. Raw German (closed captions: the only German track) and English tracks, re-aligned offline (`runs/units-c2`, 2,841 cards).
- **Judging**: one blind judge subagent per site rated 120 hovers sampled by token (`scripts/hover-packets.py`), on the build before the refinements below (`runs/l2-1-v2`); then one blind A/B per site of that first build against the final one on the tokens whose hover changed (`packets/ab-v1-v3`), and one blind A/B for the dictionary's sources (below). One judge per item: read differences under ±4 points as noise.

- **Refinements after the judged build** (judged blind on the changed tokens, about 1% of the corpus; good on those tokens before → after): YouTube 25 → 59% good, 30 → 8% wrong, preferred after : before 48 : 21; articles 32 → 65% good, 39 → 16% wrong or misleading, 19 : 5. - **Wrong hovers** (12 of 240 judged on the first build): a separable verb whose particle the hover does not join (an in "angucken", auf in "aufzählen", "dazu geben" written apart), a homograph's other word (as → As, soll → Soll: fixed by the postposition rule; Haaren → haaren: fixed by `capitalNoun`), a name given a word's meaning (Pro in "iPad Pro", Land as a surname), mal shown as "times", nächste → nah.
- **Unknown tokens** (5,067, 9.2%; French 3.5%): 3,761 capitalised (names and places: Berlin, Deutschland, Japan, Hans; kaikki gives place names proper-noun entries only), compounds (1,533 tokens split into two vocabulary words: Bahnsystem, Supermond, Pizzateig, Lichtenergie, Bundespräsidentin, Verdauungsenzyme), abbreviations (z.B, bzw, KI, SRF, USA), English words (the, of, yeah), ASR damage. German writes compounds as one word, so the vocabulary from a frequency list reaches far fewer of a text's words than in French or Spanish.
- **The polite Sie**: about 90 capitalised "Sie", "Ihre", "Ihnen" inside sentences show "she", "her", "them": the key "sie" has both headwords' senses, the lowercase one's first, and the defs dictionary does not read the written case (L2-2).
- **Separable verbs**: 207 tokens read as the separable verb whose particle closes their clause (sieht … aus → aussehen, kommt … an → ankommen, hängt … ab → abhängen); of 30 sampled, 27 are right (two from ASR fragments, "hör auf … zu" read as zuhören).

## German grammar (`core/tagging/de.ts`)
- An auxiliary is a form of haben, sein or werden with a participle or infinitive anywhere in its clause (between pauses; German puts the verb's other parts at the clause's end, or the finite verb there in a subordinate clause) and no other auxiliary or modal between them: haben + participle, werden + participle or infinitive, sein + the participle of a verb that takes sein (a closed class of motion and change-of-state verbs, with separable particles: angekommen). Sein + another participle is a state, read as the adjective ("ist geschlossen"). A modal keeps its own meaning; its infinitive is a verb.
- A separable verb's finite form is that verb where its particle closes the clause (tagged PART.Vz, or the clause's last word as a preposition or adverb) and the pack lists particle + its simple verb among its bases; without the particle it is the simple verb ("wir essen", not aufessen; "rufe", rufen, not the noun Ruf).
- Closed classes: a modal's form is the modal whatever the tag ("Kannst du"); a form of haben, sein or werden tagged as a verb is that verb ("habe", not Habe); "sein" before a noun or adjective is the possessive, at a clause's end the verb; a clause's first word before a subject pronoun is a verb ("Wissen Sie …?"); an infinitive after a determiner or preposition is a noun ("das Essen"); a capitalised word inside a sentence is a noun ("mit Haaren": Haar); the contractions (`GERMAN_RULES.contractions`) are contractions, "am" before a superlative its particle.
- CORE: rule A1 takes `WordUse.base` when it is the word itself, or a separable verb (`WordUse.separable`), whatever the pack calls its main part of speech (Aussehen, the noun, is commoner than aussehen); rule 1b reads the grammar's part of speech where no tag is trusted; `TaggedWord.capital` says how a word was written (unknown in capitals). English, Spanish and French hovers are unchanged, row for row (157,458, 67,069 and 78,973 rows).

## What a new learning language needs (German, after L2-1)
- [x] Word pattern and tokenizer: no elisions; ß and umlauts are letters; contractions am, im, zum, zur, beim, ans, ins, vom (`GERMAN_RULES.contractions`); hyphens split ("Corona-Pandemie": both parts known)
- [x] Pronominal verbs (L2-3): "sich" anywhere in the clause, the subject's own reflexive pronoun (mich with ich), an imperative's (Beeil dich)
- [x] Accents in capitals: none (German writes its umlauts in capitals)
- [x] Adjectives before nouns
- [x] Tagger and grammar: GSD only (HDT's text is academic-only); auxiliaries anywhere in the clause; separable verbs by their particle
- [x] Lemmas: every form, capitalised headwords' too, separable verbs' forms written apart, the treebank's order
- [x] Vocabulary: alt-of senses, head-line labels; case kept in the frequency list
- [x] Compounds (L2-2): an unknown word read as its known parts, keyed by its head's base, its parts' meanings joined
- [x] Names (L2-2): rule D by given names, surnames after a given name, no name after a noun phrase's opener; names that would read as compounds in the lexicon
- [x] Written case (L2-2): a capitalised headword's senses marked, shown first where the page capitalises the word (the polite Sie), and the card's headword capitalised
- [x] Phrases (L2-3): German word order (the finite verb moved before a verb pattern's other words, its particle after them), "zu" before the infinitive, the English edition's examples for the score

## L2-2 names and vocabulary (2026-11-20)
On the C2 corpus (70,910 word tokens, Netflix included), against main after C2; `de` 2026.1119.1 (format 1, 7.16 MB raw), `de-en` 2026.1119.1 (4.23 MB, 4 files; 3.93 before), published by `packs.yml` after the merge. The rebuild takes L1-5's shared build rules too (1,788 hovers, judged 48 : 5 in L1-5). Built ([architecture](../specs/architecture.md) "Closed compounds", "Names and written case"):
- **Compounds** (CORE `core/lexicon/compounds.ts`, LEARNING `COMPOUND_RULES.de`): a word the language does not know is read as its longest known head, a noun or an adjective, after a modifier that is a word, a verb's stem ("Schreib-tisch"), a noun with a linking -es-, -en-, -er-, -s-, -n-, -e- or without its final e ("Schul-buch"), or a compound itself ("Weltklima-konferenz"); parts of three letters at least. Capitalised, as German writes nouns, its head is the noun among the head's bases ("Lösungsangeboten": Angebot) and a modifier spelt like a verb is the noun ("Schienen-": Schiene), but an adjective stays as written ("Großprojekt": groß); a word that is also a verb heads one only by a form at least as long as it ("Eilles" is no compound of "les"). Its key is the modifier as written and the head's base ("verdauungsenzym"), it ranks as its rarest part (as hyphenated compounds do), and the card shows "Verdauungsenzyme → Verdauung + Enzym" as its note, its parts' first meanings joined ("digestion + enzyme"), then the head's second line; the window shows the head's senses. In a punctuated mixed-case document a lower-case word with a noun head stays unknown ("summende", "alchemistische", "agierende": a participle or adjective, not summen + Ende); speech recognition without punctuation, which writes many nouns in lower case, keeps them ("kinderfahrradsitze"). Names that would read as compounds (kaikki German's proper names: Deutschland, Frankreich, Lehmann, Wolfenstein) join the names lexicon (1,908 → 3,724 names, 16 KB gzipped) so that they do not.
- **Written case** (CORE `createDefsDictionary`, LEARNING `nounsCapitalised`; data `CAPITAL_USE`): a key a capitalised headword shares with a lowercase one marks the capitalised headword's senses "capital" where the examples write the key capitalised inside a sentence at least once for 40 times in lower case (Sie 1 : 8, Ihr 1 : 23, Reich; not Ja 1 : 81, Aber 1 : 900, Heute). A German word capitalised in a way that counts (`capitalHere`) asks for them first and keeps its tag's reading: Sie, Ihnen, Ihre inside a sentence → "you (polite)", "your"; Reich → empire, Wild → game, Gehorsam → obedience. The card writes the headword capitalised when its first line is a capitalised headword's (Vater, Kind, Essen as the noun; essen as the verb), also a compound's (Bahnsystem).
- **Names** (CORE `core/names.ts` with `nounsCapitalised`; rule D on for German): a capital says nothing by itself, so a word is a name by itself only as a given name, as written (Dietrich, Jasmin, Marie, Johannes, Jürgen; not Wolken, a plural the lexicon also lists), and not after a noun phrase's opener, which in German comes before the adjectives (a determiner or its form, a numeral, a preposition joined to the article: "Der blaue Engel", "im Ernst", "keine Taube"); a word after a person's name is theirs when the lexicon has it as a person's name or it is no noun (Albert Einstein, Jürgen Klopp, Robert Kohl, Harry Styles), document-wide only when rare as a common word (Einstein, Kohl), else only there ("Franz Josef Kaiser", not "Kaiser Franz Joseph"); a word in lower case is never one. Places and surnames by themselves stay words (Klima, Hanf, Gouda are surnames and places too).
- **Grammar** (`core/tagging/de.ts`): a modal with its infinitive in the clause is an auxiliary, so rule A2 shows the senses kaikki tags auxiliary ("kann … schwimmen": "to be able, to have the possibility of", "muss … gehen": "to have to (do something)"; "Ich kann Deutsch" keeps "to be able to do something implied"), as French pouvoir and devoir since L1-5; an adjective-tagged participle at the end of a clause of haben is the verb's (haben takes no predicative adjective: "haben dich sehr vermisst" → haben "forms the perfect aspect", vermissen "to miss").
- **Measured** (`steps/l2-2/`, `de-before` → `de-v7`): unknown tokens 6,476 → 5,055 (9.1 → 7.1%; YouTube 7.9 → 6.1%, Netflix 8.9 → 8.0%, articles 14.2 → 10.0%); 1,375 compound tokens gain a meaning (YouTube 776, articles 463, Netflix 136); 96 names (Netflix 42, articles 32, YouTube 22), all of which had a word's meaning; the polite pronouns 132; modals 495; haben + participle 17. New per 1,000 tokens 104.4 → 116.2 (compounds with a part beyond the known level; YouTube 105.3, Netflix 92.1, articles 193.0). Lines over the cap 9 → 9; classifying 6.4 → 6.1 ms per 1,000 tokens. English, Spanish and French: no hover changes before the L1-5 merge (152,902, 68,297, 74,982 rows); every German rule is gated on `nounsCapitalised` or `COMPOUND_RULES`.
- **Judged blind** (one judge per packet, Netflix in its own; each round's items left out of the next): the compound card on 150 compounds, three presentations each: the parts' meanings joined good 50–62% (wrong or misleading 22–26%), each part's meaning on a line of its own 50–70% (26–34%), the head's meaning alone 0% good but 84–98% acceptable; preferred joined 58, lines 60, head 26: joined taken (one line, fewer misleading, mirrors the note).
